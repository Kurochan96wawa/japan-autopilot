Understanding Medical Insurance and Costs for Visitors

Here’s the plain truth about medical costs in Japan for visitors: if you do not have Japanese public health insurance, you will be responsible for 100% of your medical expenses. This is why comprehensive travel insurance covering medical emergencies is not just recommended, but essential for families traveling to Japan.

Hospitals often have specific charging structures for patients without Japanese insurance. For example, the Osaka City General Medical Center charges ¥20 (excluding tax) per medical point for non-Japanese insured patients. This rate is notably higher than for those covered by local insurance, reflecting the lack of subsidy. (As of 2026, confirm on the official site for current rates).

You might encounter information about local government subsidies for child medical expenses in places like Shinjuku Ward (Tokyo), Osaka City, or Sapporo City. These programs, which offer reduced or no out-of-pocket expenses for children up to 18 years old, are typically available only to residents with Japanese health insurance. For instance, Shinjuku Ward provides a medical certificate (医療証) for eligible residents, allowing no out-of-pocket costs for insured treatments within Tokyo. Osaka City, as of April 2024, has removed income limits, making all children up to 18 eligible for a subsidy, capping daily out-of-pocket costs at ¥500 per institution (limited to two days per month), with pharmacy fees for external prescriptions being free. Sapporo City offers a "pink card" for eligible children, meaning a ¥580 first medical visit fee and ¥510 for a first dental visit, with follow-up and prescriptions free. These are fantastic for local families, but unfortunately, they do not extend to foreign tourists. Therefore, your robust travel insurance policy is your best friend.

Emergency Care for Children: What to Do

A child's sudden illness or injury can turn a trip upside down. Luckily, Japan offers several dedicated services to help parents navigate these moments.

#8000 Pediatric Emergency Telephone Consultation (小児救急電話相談)

This is a nationwide service designed specifically for parents. If your child has symptoms and you’re unsure if a hospital visit is necessary, or if you need advice on what steps to take, this is your first call. Dial #8000 from any prefecture in Japan, and you will be automatically connected to your local pediatric emergency consultation service. Pediatricians or nurses are on hand to provide guidance.

Operating hours for this service vary by local government. For example, in Osaka Prefecture, the service is available from 7:00 PM to 8:00 AM the following morning, 365 days a year. You can call #8000 from push-tone or mobile phones, or 06-6765-3650 for rotary or IP phones. The consultation itself is free, though standard call charges apply. (As of 2026, confirm exact operating hours for your specific location on the local government’s official site).

Online Symptom Checker

For a preliminary assessment, the Japan Pediatric Society's website "Kodomo no Kyukyu (ONLINE-QQ)" offers a 24-hour symptom checklist. It's tailored for children aged 1 month to 6 years and can help you gauge the urgency of a hospital visit from the comfort of your hotel room, perhaps while your little one is (finally!) asleep after a long day of sightseeing.

Immediate Emergencies

For clear, life-threatening emergencies - think serious injuries, severe breathing difficulties, or loss of consciousness - don't hesitate. Call 119 directly. This is Japan's equivalent of 911 and will connect you to fire and ambulance services. Be prepared to explain the situation as clearly as possible, and remember that English speakers may not always be immediately available, so a translation app might be handy.

Finding a Doctor or Hospital and Reservation Steps

Most hospitals and even many smaller clinics in Japan, particularly for specialized care like pediatrics, often require appointments. Trying to walk in without one can lead to very long waits, or even being turned away. It’s always best to check the hospital’s website or call them in advance to confirm their reservation requirements.

Some clinics, particularly in major cities, cater to international patients and may have specific booking procedures. For example, Omorimachi Ekimae Internal Medicine & Pediatrics Clinic in Tokyo's Ota Ward specifically requires foreign tourists to make an online appointment in advance; walk-ins are simply not accepted. If your child has a chronic condition or you’re bringing overseas medications, a referral letter from your home doctor in Japanese or English can be incredibly helpful for Japanese medical staff. Finding the right medical facility should be part of your trip planning. FAQ

What if my child needs specific medication?

Always bring a sufficient supply of any essential prescription medications your child needs, along with a doctor's note (ideally translated into Japanese). While pharmacies are widely available, Japanese equivalents might not be exact, and some specific medications may not be available or require a local prescription. Check Japan's customs rules for bringing medications before you travel.

Do Japanese doctors speak English?

While larger hospitals in major cities may have English-speaking staff or access to translation services, it's not guaranteed at every clinic, especially in more rural areas. Having a translation app on your phone can be a lifesaver.

How do I find an English-speaking doctor or clinic?

Your embassy or consulate in Japan can often provide lists of English-speaking medical facilities. Many major hotels also have resources to help you find one. Online search engines with terms like "Tokyo English speaking pediatrician" are also a good starting point.

What should I do for a minor injury like a cut or scrape?

For very minor issues, a well-stocked travel first-aid kit is your best friend. For anything requiring more than a bandage, use the #8000 service for advice or seek out a local clinic. Japan has excellent pharmacies (kusuri-ya or yakkyoku) where you can buy over-the-counter antiseptics, bandages, and minor pain relievers.

Can I just walk into any clinic?

While smaller local clinics (診療所, shinryojo) might accept walk-ins for minor issues, many specialized clinics and larger hospitals require appointments. Always try to call ahead or check their website to confirm, especially if you're traveling in a busy area like Tokyo or Kyoto.

Is travel insurance essential for family trips to Japan?

Absolutely. As visitors without Japanese health insurance are responsible for 100% of medical costs, robust travel insurance with comprehensive medical coverage is non-negotiable. Ensure it covers emergency medical treatment, evacuation, and repatriation.

What if my child has allergies?

Carry an allergy card translated into Japanese clearly stating your child's allergies. When dining out, show it to restaurant staff. For severe allergies, ensure you have an EpiPen (with a doctor's note) and understand how to administer it. Always consult your pediatrician before travel for specific advice regarding your child's allergies in a foreign country.

How can I prepare before I travel?

Beyond travel insurance, pack a good first-aid kit, note down the #8000 number and your nearest embassy's contact. Research any hospitals near your accommodations, especially in major cities, and save their contact details. Being prepared takes a weight off your shoulders, even if you hope you never need it.

Being prepared for your child's health needs in Japan means you can enjoy your adventures with much greater peace of mind, knowing that help is always just a phone call away.
